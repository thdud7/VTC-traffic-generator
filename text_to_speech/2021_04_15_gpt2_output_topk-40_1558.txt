The Commission also noted that the Dutch government did not consult the Commission, while instead "obtained information about the work to be carried out from Deloitte". It said Deloitte ignored a July 5 deadline for submitting draft proposals.

Belgium is the fourth country to carry out a national audit of its financial markets in response to the European Commission's ongoing investigation into whether national regulators are sufficiently tough in their rules and tough enough in the way they impose fines on bank wrongdoing.

'Tough on crime'

Commission vice-president Frans Timmermans said Belgium had to demonstrate an alternative approach to managing financial markets, which was not "tough on crime".

"Our task today is to provide a clear and coherent policy for the future in the EU in the area of bank supervision and in particular of bank supervisors," Timmermans said.

Deloitte has said the report will draw on research it has conducted over the last two years with experts in France, Germany and Portugal.

"It is about trying to clarify the existing framework," Deloitte's chief analyst, Eric O'Neill said of the report.

In Belgium's response, the report said the financial system's risk model did not work as well for smaller firms as it had for large ones, that the financial system should be "tighter", and that national regulations should be strengthened.

"Although it is encouraging that the recommendations in the proposed report have already been adopted in other countries, they are only the start," Timmermans's draft said on Wednesday of the Belgian report. "We expect Belgium to put in place a comprehensive reform package in a timely manner, and to implement the recommendations, especially those about a greater role for national supervisory authorities."

The Department of Veterans Affairs released its 2016 National Suicide Prevention Strategy Thursday, which included a number of changes to how to go about providing the health care provided to its 11 million Americans.

The strategy calls for a "comprehensive reorientation" of the VA to "strengthen the overall health and wellness of America's Veterans and their families."

That includes, among other things:

An expanded use of telehealth. Telemedicine is "used by 75% of all U.S. VA providers and by 90% of the VHA's private sector hospitals," as The Washington Post reported on Thursday. The VA health system's Telehealth Services Division (TDS) is expected to "more than double its staff of 50 telehealth specialists" this year to 150.

VHA Secretary David Shulkin said in December that it was "a critical opportunity to ensure our veterans receive the services and support they need," though it has yet to be made official.

Another major step: the ability to remotely screen patients. This would allow the VA to determine if there is an urgent problem with someone's health and take action — and would take advantage of the Affordable Care Act's ability to save money on care.

The strategy includes increased funding for suicide prevention programs, a major investment in technology to handle the influx of electronic health records and more efforts to provide "best value care" for veterans, particularly the uninsured.

The strategy also calls for reducing delays in care, increasing access to mental health care and increasing support for mental health providers.

The VA's efforts may have a number of benefits for veterans looking for treatment, as The Huffington Post's Michael McAuliff reported in October.

"One concern the president articulated was, given the VA's massive size, that veterans might be waiting for treatment that they need," McAuliff explained. "The administration is also taking steps to try to keep VA providers from charging too much for care while trying to rein in costs, to make sure that their doctors, nurses and clinics can keep up with the demands of an aging health care system."

Lately it seems that I'm being haunted by an invisible ghost. It is called the Fermi paradox, which describes the question: how do we know that we're alone in the universe, and that there isn't life elsewhere?

The original Fermi paradox appeared in the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists when physicist Enrico Fermi was asked to determine exactly what the implications of the idea of a "multiverse" could be. The question in itself was intriguing, but the answer had many implications: there may be an infinite number of parallel universes, there might not be life in the universe, etc.

In the years since that time, some have become increasingly frustrated by the lack of response by Fermi to the question of the multiverse. The multiverse is now regarded as a "myth." So I figured now would be a good time to give an updated version of "The Great Invisibility" to the wider Universe.

The Fermi Paradox

One of the implications of Fermi's paradox is that, if we've found life elsewhere, it must be in the form we have been looking for it: intelligent life. If intelligent life exists, and we've found it, then we must look elsewhere for it—beyond the edge of our solar system.

Another implication is that, if there is intelligent life somewhere else on Earth, we must search for it. To search there, we must travel.

There are a few different paths we could pursue:

Searching within the solar system, to the nearest star systems

Searching in space

Searching on Earth

Searching near alien solar systems

Searching on the Moon (or Mars)

Using telescopes

Using radio frequencies

However, these paths all have challenges:

All these activities require massive amounts of energy (explosives, radio frequency transmissions, etc). The space age has made space much less hospitable to us. The universe is filled with billions of supermassive black holes and other enormous gravitational objects. The Earth's radio spectrum has more than a hundred billion billion oscillations per second, but we can only see a fraction of that. These oscillations are produced by the rotation of the Earth. To detect the Earth and then use that information to detect supermassive black holes on some distant planet requires more than a century for such transmissions to be converted into light. Even in the next century, it will take at least a million years to transmit light. That's a long time.

If we choose to search for intelligent life elsewhere in the universe, we might be able to use Fermi's paradox to detect it. However, this requires a lot of energy.

It's also important to note that, based upon Fermi's paradox, finding that intelligent-life-on-site is possible is not a "sure thing."

The Fermi Paradox and "Star Trek":

The Fermi paradox might be viewed as a paradox in an alternate version of "Star Trek." Instead of looking around the solar system and finding intelligent life, it would be more natural to look in space. The stars that are brighter might show more life (it is thought that, in any galaxy, there will be many more stars brighter than us, which means there is more life there), but it might be possible to see alien technology such as star ships. Even intelligent life (such as humans?) might be found elsewhere. The "Star Trek" version of the Fermi paradox might be a case where a new species is encountered in the "Prime Directive" and is forbidden to contact Earth; otherwise the Fermi paradox could be taken as the "real" Fermi paradox.

One of the great difficulties in solving this "fugitive" paradox is that, while there is a simple mathematical formula for "if x is the universe and we have observed an x-event, then the observer must have observed an x." That implies that, for something like intelligent life, we can know that we have created x—we only need to be able to prove it.

"The question is not whether the system is corrupt; it is by what point it must be reformed" – Ralph Nader(2)

Voting machine fraud is as old as the first lever used to depress the voter's lever, but it didn't become a matter of significant concern until the 1960s and 1970s with the advent of electronic voting, the widespread adoption of punch card voting systems, and the growing use of vote tabulation.

With the advent of the computer and computer memory, the fraud became easier to detect and harder to hide.

While it is sometimes argued that a computer's errors can be detected, there is a significant difference – whether or not a computer can reproduce a given result. Errors – whether those of humans, programming errors, glitches or bugs – can occur. Errors are inevitable during the use of any new system, and a "perfect" system should not be required to produce a 100% error free outcome.

Despite this, modern computerized voting systems have been touted as "easy to implement," without ever actually proving they are easy to implement.

A number of studies have shown that computerized systems are vulnerable to hacking.

The recent election in Ohio raised the alarm about the vulnerability of Ohio's election systems and how they could be hacked. The State recently issued a series of directives to state agencies for updating their election systems and implementing new cyber security procedures.

Although the U.S. Department of Justice issued a bulletin in 2003 warning counties about the possibility of tampering with their electronic voting systems, the Ohio Board of Elections and the U.S. State Department had not yet issued their own advisories.

Despite the warnings issued by officials with both the U.S. Department of Justice and those of the Ohio Board of Elections, no state has moved to mandate computerized voting.

In fact, the same agencies which issued the computer vulnerabilities warnings are the same agencies tasked with overseeing publically-funded elections for the citizens of America.

In recent events in Virginia, Secretary of the Commonwealth Board of Elections Ken Martin, a Democrat, made the astonishing claim that there was "no way to hack the electronic [voting] system."

One cannot argue that there is "no way" to hack a computer, but even he conceded that there is "no way" to hack the results of an election in Virginia, given that the only reason why electronic voting systems are in place is to prevent vote fraud.

With electronic voting systems already at risk for fraud, the question is whether or not the integrity of electronic voting in Virginia can be salvaged by implementing computerized voting systems for elections.

The question is also why a computerized system can't be employed with the same confidence that people who vote in person can rely on the integrity of voting in person.

The lack of confidence can be found in an article entitled, "Voting Machines: What are they and how reliable are they?" published in the September-October 2006 issue of The Washington Post; "One year after new voting machines went on sale in Virginia, there's little reason to be confident that they are safe," the article stated.

In addition to numerous problems with equipment, several articles published in this period suggested a lack of confidence in the integrity of the software that is supposed to count votes and record the results of elections.

At other times, it has been suggested that even if the software is used exactly as intended, there would still be the potential for the vote total to be different if there is cheating in the polling stations or election officials.

This is especially true since the "software" is run entirely by computers, so the results could be compromised if people who don't use computers are counted in the results.

It was not until a 2008 survey conducted by political scientist John Sides that a significant portion of the public was presented with data on which to base their confidence in the integrity of voting machines.

As previously reported by Reddit and others, there is a game called League of Legends which allows players to play against other players of the same region/plat rank and level. A common way to do those things is to play with a player in his personal lane as a support player while taking on a player with a higher level in a more dangerous lane. If you're a very good ADC with an advantage, you might want to take on an enemy with a better ADC in that lane even if he's your teammate.

The US Supreme Court on Tuesday affirmed a lower-court decision to strike down a key component of Texas' "show your papers" law, which requires law-abiding Texans to carry government-issued identification to gain access to government buildings or facilities, including airports.

The law—which Texas' federal judges found unconstitutional over the summer—was adopted by the Texas Legislature in 2010 after the United States Supreme Court struck down its entire immigration law in 2011. In an effort to reduce illegal immigration, the Texas legislature enacted a law that required identification to gain access to state-run buildings. The Supreme Court later blocked the law in its entirety, but struck down a portion of it that required "show your papers" to enter certain state buildings, including the Capitol.

While the government generally doesn't require citizens to carry government-issued identification in order to obtain various government goods and services, the Supreme Court ruled that section of the Texas law—specifically, section 8 of the law—made that a requirement to enter the state Capitol. While a majority of the court agreed that the "show your papers" provision of the law is a "reasonable" restriction on the constitutional freedom of movement of immigrants and refugees (read: illegal immigrants), Chief Justice John Roberts wrote that the restriction "is so unrelated to immigration concerns that it bears no rational correlation to immigration enforcement."[1]

But for the Texas legislators, with their support of the law, the court upheld the law's entirety.

The court did grant an injunction to the Texas Government Accountability Board (TGB) to allow the agency to carry out the requirement, which means that undocumented immigrants—as well as legal Mexican minors and people of color—whose identification shows they are lawfully present in the United States must present identification to get into public facilities regardless of whether the agency has reasonable suspicion or probable cause that they are in the country illegally.

"The court is not saying that immigration agents need a 'reason to suspect' they are in the country illegally from the moment they see the individual's ID," writes Stephen Yale-Loehr on the website for the Texas Freedom Network. "But if they do and they are reasonably suspicious—at least, in theory—then they should be able to stop and question them at the building to determine their legal status and what, if anything, they might be carrying."

The injunction, while temporary, will affect an undetermined number of undocumented people—the exact numbers aren't yet known—because the court's order is for the district attorney of the Travis County, where much of the law's enforcement activities are centered. The TGB was given 30 days to make sure that each individual who is unable to comply with the law's requirement presents proper identification to get into public buildings including the state Capitol.

As Loehr points out, the state is already making it harder for undocumented immigrants to get into government buildings by increasing the cost for IDs—with the cost increased between $8.50 and $19.50 for a 10-year card or a $50,000 card—to as high as $95.

"[T]here is no justification or legal path to obtain these kinds of cards now from the TGB," Loehr writes. "Even if the TGB were willing to issue a discount card for those who pay the cost, many undocumented immigrants still cannot afford to buy and travel thousands of miles to obtain a new identification card."

While the Supreme Court upheld the law's overall provisions, the court also struck down a portion of it that authorized police to use a person's immigration status as a reason to stop them and question them for whatever reason—such as suspected drug use—in Texas. The requirement, which the court found unconstitutional over the summer, was the same type of "show your papers" provision passed by the Texas legislature.

The decision to strike down the unconstitutional portion of the law was unanimous, and comes on the heels of a decision in November by the 4th US Circuit Court of Appeals striking down the law's "show your papers" portion of the law for being onerous and ineffective, along with numerous court decisions on the issue.

The Texas Legislature is now in the process of passing a law to remove the "show your papers" provision from the law that went into effect immediately after the Supreme Court's decision in January. Texas Gov. Greg Abbott is expected to sign the bill.

"This is a real win for the law's challengers," Loehr writes. "They have been blocked from enforcing that particular provision based on a completely irrational idea that because they are immigrants they should be treated that way."

When the Senate's version of the bill is brought before the house of commons for votes this week and next, the Conservatives will be well and truly set to block the legislation.

However, that may not be the case quite yet – the NDP may not have the votes to bring the bill into the house, and in the event that fails the Liberal Party in the House of Commons could decide to bring it in. In that event, the bill's fate and the fate of thousands of Canadians could be in the hands, in a very small way, of the Liberal Party in the House of Commons – and that's why we're planning to flood them with phone calls and emails this week.

We all know the deal – the Conservatives' plan to shut down health-care access to millions of Canada's poorest residents over six years will involve the elimination of the universal, free access to basic health care that Canadians receive from the provinces.

This means that at least one in ten Canadians, with the number likely to be much higher, will lose access to an essential service.

The bill's provisions on how to handle long waits for access to emergency patients, and how many of those Canadians can wait up to a year for a first visit to the doctor at a hospital are not a serious concern of either the NDP or Liberals, according to NDP health critic Peter Julian.

"What they're actually getting at here is really a way to kill the universal system and bring back the Canada Health Act, which is a very bad system with a horrible history in this country," Julian explained. "I don't know how we can take anything they're offering seriously, because I don't think the Canada Health Act has any place in a modern, compassionate and humane society like this."

The Canada Health Act – named after the long-time Conservative senator who led and fought for Canada's health system from 1958 to 1986 – was an effort by Prime Minister Pierre Trudeau to create a national government-operated health system that would focus on treating Canadian patients, as opposed to provincial governments. The system was largely responsible for the country's high per capita health-care budgets, a legacy that was never truly broken.

The move toward a more nationalized service was also responsible for the creation of a federal Department of Health, which would oversee most of the provinces, and the development of the government's annual National Health Expenditure Report.

However, in the mid-1990s the Conservative Party decided to take a different approach with health care – rather than seeing hospitals and public insurance as an option between the provinces, they set out to eliminate Canada's health system – and with it its many social programs and services – entirely.

Over the course of the next 13 years, Conservative governments systematically dismantled Canada's social safety net by defunding and dismantling programs like the Canada Pension Plan, disability benefits, maternal and child health services, Canada's international aid program, environmental laws, programs to combat poverty and homelessness, and more.

The Harper administration's plan was to eliminate the social welfare system, and to instead spend taxpayer dollars on health care, as a right for Canadians – not a necessity.

The cost of the Harper government's crusade to completely end social welfare was devastating. In fact, there was even public outcry in Canada after revelations of the scandal that led to the deaths of two young women as a result of sub-par care at a Toronto hospital.

Although Canada's health care system was in a much better place before the Conservative governments came to power, it was never fully reinstated after the Conservatives took power – at least until the Liberals came to power and brought in the Universal Health Care Act (which was passed in 2008 by a vote in the House of Commons) after years of back-room discussions and negotiations.

Under the bill, the Canada Health Act would be replaced by an expanded Canada Health Transfer (CHT), a form of federal cash which is handed out to the provinces and territories, with annual payments ranging between $5.8 billion to $33 billion over six years.

Under the current Canada Health Act, all people in Canada who meet basic needs must pay out of pocket. While the Liberals recently announced new spending on health care in the form of CHT for the provinces starting in 2017/18, some provinces, including B.C. and Manitoba, have yet to make the payments.

The bill also proposes that each province be given a fixed amount of funds to spend on health care, and that the costs of care in the province are to be deducted from the federal amount.

Under the Liberals, the money would come out of our pocket, meaning that we would not be paying for some of the things we currently spend our money on, which includes things like home care, prescription drugs and hospital bills

"They're basically saying that we're paying now because we should," Julian said. With no money coming out of our pockets, he added, health care would remain primarily a provincial responsibility.

A recent study by the Center for Immigration Studies (CIS) found that most undocumented immigrants come to the United States for high-level professional or professional school, and not to compete against American students in the labor market, as some critics allege. "Undocumented immigrants in the United States pay taxes, including income taxes, payroll taxes, property taxes and excise taxes," the study states, "because they pay in some but not all of these U.S. taxes due to their undocumented status and other constraints. Most are likely paying some or all of these U.S. taxes because their wages and labor costs provide them with an income which is higher than would be earned by any lawful resident."

The CIS study concludes that undocumented immigrants, especially those who enter the United States for the education and professional fields, are "often in the higher-skilled jobs or are members of advanced graduate schools." The study notes that "[t]here is very little evidence suggesting that U.S. businesses discriminate against U.S. educated workers." The report says that undocumented immigrants are far more "productive" than the unauthorized population and the government and private entities pay higher wages to undocumented workers.

The CIS study notes that since they are not legal residents, they must pay Social Security taxes while a citizen would pay payroll taxes. "Undocumented immigrants pay an average of $1,300 more a year in Social Security taxes than do legal resident workers," the study states, "in addition to about $2,400 in federal income taxes and $8,700 in employer-tax payroll taxes."

"In 2013, undocumented immigrants contributed $2.3 trillion to the nation's economy, $2.6 trillion in wages and $6.5 trillion in capital income taxes," the CIS study states. "Undocumented immigrants also provide many of the benefits of the U.S. tax base, and they pay about $818 billion in sales taxes through the use of tax credits and other forms of government assistance, primarily in the form of food stamps."

The CIS report notes how undocumented immigrants pay their way even though they are more likely to be poor than the official population. "The poverty rate for legal immigrants was 11% in 2013, versus 12% for undocumented immigrants, and is lower among legal immigrants (20%) than the unauthorized (22%) and unauthorized who have lived in the country more than five years (23%)."

The CIS study states that the reason why fewer illegal immigrants are coming to the United States is that they cannot find jobs due to the economic stimulus measures implemented under the Obama administration; for instance, President Obama implemented more rules and permits for people to work than during all previous administrations combined.

CIS also notes that "it was the enactment of the 2012 economic stimulus package that encouraged more legal immigration." The study notes that "it is unlikely that any such economic stimulus can stimulate the economy when a significant number of the new legal immigrants do not arrive until late 2015-early 2016" because most illegal aliens are not included in the calculation for the unemployment rate. The CIS study also notes that "it is likely that many illegal aliens would like to work and could be employed under the new business and agricultural incentives in place during the 2012-2013 period," making the numbers of illegal immigrants more manageable, in addition to the fact that "there is no evidence showing that employers have in any way restricted employment opportunities or wages" for illegal aliens.

In addition, the study notes that some of the economic stimulus created by the Obama administration came thanks to the fact that "illegal aliens and U.S.-born children were allowed to pay in-state tuition rates to the universities in California and New York that received huge percentages of their funding through federal programs (Federal Pell grants, etc.)."

"The administration has also used executive action in some areas to expand access to legal residency," the CIS study notes. "While illegal immigrants generally pay taxes, they do a great deal of business within the United States because it provides jobs that they would otherwise not have been able to obtain. The study found that many businesses prefer to employ U.S.-born workers (even those who entered the country illegally) because of their superior skills."

"There are about 11.2 million illegal immigrants who are likely paid some amount of state, local, federal, or foreign income taxes," the report says. "The estimated total of these taxes is $2.3 trillion, or about 17 percent of gross domestic product. They account for about one-half of all private payroll taxes paid by the entire population."

CIS says the study also shows that when illegal immigrants are counted "the illegal immigrant population is closer to half the U.S. general population than many of those opposing amnesty will have you believe."

In December 2000, the world's richest men gathered at Davos, Switzerland, to decide how best to fight poverty, the world's biggest issue, by creating an international community to do the same.

The International Monetary Fund (IMF) had just announced it would take over responsibility for overseeing how the rich could avoid giving the poor all they owned.

Yet, on the next day, as the meeting began at 11.30am, the world's worst poverty disaster – the Indian Ocean tsunami – hit. As it turned out, by the time the meeting started it was too late to agree on a response, even if it had been possible.

The IMF's then-president, Dominique Strauss-Kahn, and its then-director Paul Wolfowitz – both members of the Davos elite – took part in Davos. So did two more former IMF managing directors – Javier Solana and James Wolfensohn, both members of the World Economic Forum.

Yet, despite being the IMF's most powerful figures, they failed to get a response from anyone at the meeting to the call for action.

The failure to come up with an agreement was one of the key problems in the decade leading up to the financial crash of 2007. It was a disaster for the world economy, which was left with huge debts and huge deficits.

The IMF was created as a rescue agency, but its role quickly became that of economic enforcer, and over the years, it played a major role in the creation of austerity, bailouts and economic reforms across the world.

The IMF also came up with a range of other schemes, including a new development bank in the United States as well as hundreds of millions of dollars in loans with which to fight poverty, often at the expense of economic freedom. It came up with a number of rules that were intended to ensure that the rich got wealthier and everyone else struggled to do the same.

One of the most notorious was the so-called Doha Development Agenda, the so-called "D" for deficit. In the post-war period, this was the rule that the richer you were, the sooner the poor should have their resources redistributed to tackle their poverty.

When in 2000 the IMF first announced its intention to take over responsibility for managing the distribution of the poor, an organisation dedicated to reducing global poverty called the Centre for Policy Alternatives launched a campaign to ensure the new rules did not change.

The campaign focused particularly on the impact of the Doha rules on what were known as the "lucky countries", wealthy countries which did not want to contribute to the "lucky countries" of the poor countries. The centre was concerned the new rules would lead to a redistribution of wealth from the lucky countries to the not-so-lucky countries. The Supreme Court struck down the part of the Voting Rights Act that required states with a history of racial discrimination to get federal approval before changing their voting laws.

For generations, voting laws in the U.S. have been dominated by the federal government's requirement that states and localities get preapproval from the federal government before changing their voting laws and standards, commonly called the Voting Rights Act.

The 1965 VRA, passed in a moment called the "southern surge" when Jim Crow segregation was being dismantled in states from South Carolina to Texas under the threat of federal law, placed a heavy burden on states in terms of finding new ways to meet their voter ID and other voting requirements. Under the preclearance formula the federal government and most states use, states with a history of racial discrimination must provide information to the Department of Justice and federal court that is needed to make sure that their voting laws adequately respect the rights of eligible voters. But the preclearance requirement came with federal approval that was supposed to lead to improved voting systems, not a ban on voting laws, so after the passage of the 1965 Act states were allowed to move ahead with voting changes after receiving federal approval. The Supreme Court today rejected that system, instead opting to give the Department of Justice more power in enforcing the act and giving federal preclearance of any future changes.

"Today's decision takes us back to a shameful era, when laws based on race or ethnicity would prevent citizens of one state or a local jurisdiction from registering to vote in another," said New York Attorney General Eric Schneiderman in a statement announcing today's court decision.

In order to achieve all-state status for the San Bernardino National Forest, two areas of the land must receive the following types of land designations before construction can begin on any of them:

1. High-use areas containing a specified number of dwellings

2. State or Federal recreational/adventure areas.

In areas designated for high-use areas and high-intensity forest fire suppression, the National Forest Service will provide technical advice to landowners and communities when constructing roads, trails, and other facilities. High-use, developed recreation areas are areas that support the highest levels of recreational activities. The National Forests' goal for their high-use areas is that the highest level of public use is provided in the shortest possible time that minimizes impacts to the forest. For instance, while a large recreational area can support a large number of activities, a low-intensity area could support fewer activities and still make a tremendous impact to the forest through an overall reduction in impacts to the ecosystem and forest resources. High-use areas are not intended for the construction of facilities and are restricted to areas for recreation and activities that are of high intensity for the public's benefit. Recreational/adventurous recreation areas provide recreational activities that do not produce impacts to other forest resources. Adventuring activities can be of many types, including hiking, boating, fishing, and camping, and are generally not of high intensity for the purpose of recreation.

Recreational/adventurous recreation areas (R/A) must contain a specified number of recreation equipment sites. The number may not exceed 5% of the total number of sites within the high-use area. Permits must be issued for each site if a vehicle will access the site, including temporary campers. Permits for permanent or extended stays must originate with the local forest district office or regional or national headquarters.

Special use areas will have the same requirements to obtain permits and develop infrastructure as other high-use areas.

There are many types of special use areas that support activities of special interest which may not be included in any other high-use areas or special-use areas or where no permit is required. These include:

Cities, where the only activity permitted will have high intensity impacts on the local environment. Cities are generally not subject to the California Wilderness Act, so these special use areas will have lower environmental impact criteria than special use areas that are subject to the wilderness act, for example, recreation areas such as State Highways.

Camp sites that are located within the boundaries of the National Forest to conduct a short-term camping activity for recreational visitors. Campsites may not be constructed.

Campgrounds and other open areas with camping facilities for camping or other activities, except where such facilities are on lands of special scientific interest or where special use permits are routinely issued. Campgrounds and open areas have lower environmental impact criteria than facilities such as recreation areas which are on lands of special scientific interest where recreational use is permitted.

Lodge areas

Lodge areas are locations that have a fixed design or configuration, can be utilized by campers without being separated from wilderness areas, and are intended for the accommodation of tourists and their enjoyment of local special features such as the forest service campground, the picnic area or other facilities. Lodges may not be built or constructed except for special use applications.

Mountain Biking

While mountain biking can be extremely valuable to the Forest and beneficial to public access, we do not believe mountain biking within the National Forest limits is a "special use area". Mountain biking is not subject to the Wilderness Act and may be developed on State Highways without required permits. This is not a "special use area".

Camping

Camping sites are permitted only at campgrounds. Camp facilities are also permitted at the following locations: wilderness areas and special purpose areas, such as State Highways. Only permits and other appropriate management actions will be granted to persons and organizations wishing to operate such facilities.

Parks and Recreation Areas

In many cases, special use areas fall within the boundaries of a county recreation area or park. The San Bernardino National Forest does offer special use areas within its boundaries as well as other recreation areas that may be of interest to the public, and may have amenities such as hiking, mountain biking, camping and other similar programs available.

The San Bernardino National Forest has established an Outdoor Recreation Commission to oversee the planning, construction and maintenance of special use areas on national forest lands within its boundaries. The Outdoor Recreation Commission is a multi-sector task force composed of representatives from the Forest Service, state agencies, national park systems and other agencies participating in special use areas, and is responsible for the following:

Monitoring special-use areas;

Advising on the creation, operation and maintenance of special use areas within the Forest; and,

Advising the appropriate local government on issues related to special-use areas.

He had no formal or secondary education. His father died when his mother still a child, and his mother remarried when he was a teenager, bringing along her sister and brother.

After serving in World War I for India, he returned to Jharkhand and started his own business. It was a family business, based on manual skills and hard work – and it was a steady income. He eventually went on to found a large textile mill.

But then tragedy struck.

On June 23rd, 1921, he was on his way to work. He went over a bridge and saw a man falling in front of him.

He grabbed the fallen man and started shaking him.

"I had a good idea that they weren't going to be able to run. But I just started to shake the fallen man. I looked around for some people to talk to," he later said.

Unbelievably, the man didn't die at all. The man was just a man and it was his friend who had been hit by the same train.

His friend was taken to a clinic and died the next day. It was his lucky day. Not because he survived or even that he was unharmed – but because he knew that people would not hesitate to help a "friend" in need. That gave Jibran Shah a certain sense of power, even though he was only 24 years old. It led him on to become the first non-governmental organisation ever named as a beneficiary of the US foreign aid programme – under which the money had to be routed.

But the most important part of his story was never told.

After a week of working in a nearby factory to get his feet unstuck, Shah found that the man whose fall he had helped was back on the job. On his way back, he was knocked unconscious by a falling object. After recovering, he returned to his factory and left his post to tend to the injured woman.

When he returned, he couldn't shake the thought of being a hero. He started working on his own again.

Then came the fateful day.

When Shah took a train to get back home from the factory, he arrived to find his friend lying on the ground. "He was dead. My heart was broken," he said.

The man had suffered a devastating spinal cord injury and his death was a complete shock to the family. That is when Jibran Shah realised he would have to become a hero.

From that day he never left his factory to attend funeral services.

A small group of fellow workers followed his lead, volunteering to stay by a funeral pyre. When the family arrived to pay their respects, the workers left the funeral pyre in honour of their "friend".

The funeral pyre remained there for more than a week, a symbol of this group of workers who would always help an injured man in need – no matter how poor or uneducated his family.

On the day of his death, the man's widow made it all the way to Jibran Shah to pay her respects, and on her way, she met the widow of one of his co-workers. It was a meeting where both sisters spoke to the widow of their loved one and learned that their loved one had saved Jibran Shah's life on multiple occasions. They were both so touched by it that they decided to name Shah as the first beneficiary of US foreign aid – on his mother's behalf. There is still a plaque in his office, where workers are free to put their own photos in honour of their past.

After the woman made it to Srikakulam, he took time off to mourn his friend, and then got back on.

As for the other workers who followed in his footsteps, the Jibran Shah Museum was opened in the 1970s - but not before he did something special.

He set up The Jibran Shah Foundation – a non-governmental organisation, dedicated to supporting poor people in the country. He also donated some of the funds from his textile mill to support the foundation.

The Jibran Shah Foundation began to provide grants to local NGOs who supported local people through different programmes. It also began training teachers and giving scholarships to poor students who are pursuing higher education in rural and semi-urban areas.

Since then people have travelled from all over South India to visit The Jibran Shah Museum to remember Shah's life. There are books and memorabilia.

They have had millions of dollars invested over the last decade in educating people about how to take money out (with pre-set limits) and deposit it back into the bank, usually through prepaid debit cards. Their advertising and marketing strategy, which depends entirely on getting the consumer to buy what they are selling, is the same as the big banks' strategy: to convince you.


The big payola
